Mahalanobis D is a multivariate effect size that combines multiple standardized mean differences (Cohen's d) into a single number, accounting for the correlations between variables. Unlike simply averaging the d values, it captures the full multivariate separation between groups. When traits are correlated, D can be substantially larger or smaller than you'd expect from the individual d's alone.
Univariate effect sizes (Cohen's d)
M-FD (directionality)
M-FT (typicality)
M-FP (probability)
M-FC (centrality)
D = √(d′ R−1 d)
d is the vector of Cohen's d values and R is the pooled within-group correlation matrix. When traits are uncorrelated (R = I), D reduces to the square root of the sum of squared d's. With positive correlations and d's of mixed sign, D can exceed this value because the correlations amplify the separation along the discriminant axis.
Sex-directionality (M-FD) is the simplest M-F index: a weighted sum of standardized trait scores, where each trait is weighted by its Cohen's d. It captures the extent to which an individual's profile is shifted in the male or female direction, irrespective of how the traits correlate with each other.
M-FD distribution by sex
Trait weights in M-FD
M-FD = z′d / ‖d‖
Each trait contributes proportionally to its d value, regardless of how it correlates with other traits. This makes M-FD simple and transparent, but it does not maximize group separation — that requires accounting for the correlation structure.
Sex-typicality (M-FT) uses the discriminant function (LDA) to find the linear combination that maximally separates the groups. Unlike M-FD, it accounts for trait correlations, weighting traits not just by their d values but by their unique contribution to group separation. The two indices usually correlate strongly, but can disagree for individual profiles — producing discordant cases.
M-FT distribution by sex
M-FD vs M-FT (discordant profiles shaded)
M-FT = z′a / √(a′R−1a),   a = R−1d
Discordant profiles are individuals whose M-FD and M-FT scores have opposite signs: they are shifted in the male direction on the raw composite but classified as more female-typical by the discriminant function (or vice versa). This happens when the centroid axis and discriminant axis diverge, which increases with stronger trait correlations.
Sex-probability (M-FP) converts the typicality score into a probability of group membership via the logistic function. It is a monotonic transformation of M-FT, which compresses the tails: highly male-typical and highly female-typical profiles are pushed toward 1 and 0, while the distinction between "somewhat typical" and "very typical" is blurred.
M-FP distribution by sex
Logistic mapping: M-FT → M-FP
M-FP = exp(z′a) / (1 + exp(z′a))
When the groups are well separated (large D), the M-FP distributions become highly skewed, clustering near 0 and 1. This makes M-FP excellent for classification but less useful for distinguishing individuals within the tails. Note that M-FP uses the un-normalized discriminant score, so it is always concordant with M-FT.
Sex-centrality (M-FC) measures how close an individual is to each group's centroid using the Mahalanobis distance, rather than projecting onto an axis. A score of +1 means the profile is as close to the male centroid as the centroids are to each other; −1 means the same for the female centroid. Unlike M-FT, M-FC captures "averageness" within a group, not just position along the separating axis.
M-FC distribution by sex
M-FT vs M-FC
M-FC = (DM(z, f) − DM(z, m)) / DM(m, f)
M-FC and M-FT agree closely near the center of the distribution but diverge in the tails. M-FC is more "compressed" toward the extremes because the iso-centrality curves become increasingly non-linear far from the classification boundary. With many traits, the meaning of centrality changes: most individuals lie far from both centroids in high-dimensional space.
The aggregation tradeoff for Mahalanobis D: using more, narrower variables (items vs. facets vs. domains) increases the number of dimensions p, which inflates raw D through sampling bias. At the same time, narrower variables have lower reliability, attenuating the correlations and distorting the indices. The bias-corrected Du adjusts for the dimensionality bias.
Mahalanobis D across aggregation levels
Raw D increases with the number of variables, partly because each added variable contributes sampling noise to the distance estimate. The bias-corrected estimator Du = √max(0, D²(nm+nf−p−3)/(nm+nf−2) − p(nm+nf)/(nmnf)) removes this inflation. When reliability is low and p is large, the correction can be dramatic.
This demonstrates a genuine tradeoff: domain-level analysis is more reliable and stable, but may miss sex differences that emerge at the facet level. There is no universally "correct" level of aggregation — the choice depends on the research question.
