Causal Inference: Two Languages for One Question

Rubin's potential outcomes and Pearl's structural causal models are often presented as rival frameworks. They are not. They are two vocabularies for the same underlying problem, and the same data. Move the sliders to see how identical assumptions and identical estimators are written in both languages.
The scenario

Does an SSRI reduce depressive symptoms?

A naturalistic clinical study follows 80 adults seeking care for major depressive disorder. At intake, each patient is rated as mild-to-moderate or severe based on a structured interview — this is the baseline severity X. The treating clinician then decides, based on her own judgement and the patient's preferences, whether to start an SSRI (T = 1) or recommend watchful waiting (T = 0). After 12 weeks, symptom reduction Y (BDI-II baseline minus follow-up; positive = improvement) is recorded for everyone.

The investigators want to estimate the average treatment effect of the SSRI. The obvious move — compare mean Y in the treated group to mean Y in the untreated group — is biased, because severity drives both treatment assignment and outcome. Severe patients are more likely to be put on medication and tend to show larger reductions on follow-up (more room to improve, regression toward the mean, more attention from the clinician). The simple difference confounds drug effect with severity effect.

This is the canonical setup for which Rubin's potential outcomes framework and Pearl's SCM were each developed. Below, the same simulated data is shown both ways. The sliders control how strongly severity drives treatment assignment, and what the true SSRI effect would be in a randomized trial.

Potential Outcomes Rubin (1974)
Each dot is one patient at 12-week follow-up. ● untreated (Y observed at T=0)   ● treated (Y observed at T=1)   ● counterfactual Y the patient would have had under the other treatment (visible only in reveal mode). The fundamental problem of causal inference: we never see both columns of the same row.
ATE = E[Y(1) − Y(0)]
Stratified estimator: for each X, take E[Y|T=1,X] − E[Y|T=0,X]; weight by P(X).
Structural Causal Model Pearl (1995)
X = baseline severity  ·  T = treatment  ·  Y = symptom reduction
T := fT(X, UT)
Y := 2 + β·T + 5·X + UY
Backdoor adjustment:
P(Y | do(T=t)) = Σx P(Y | T=t, X=x)·P(X=x)

Try it. Drag confounding to 0 — assignment becomes random and the naive estimate collapses onto the truth. Push it toward 1 and the naive number drifts upward (the X-on-Y effect is leaking through T), but the adjusted estimate stays put. Click do(T = 1) on the right: the X→T arrow goes red and dashed (Pearl's "graph surgery"), and on the left every patient is now shown at the Y they would have had on the drug — exactly the counterfactual column you can reveal in observational mode.
Caveat. This is a clean linear-Gaussian world with one binary measured confounder, no interaction, no unmeasured confounding, and no missing data. Real applications rarely cooperate. The hard part of causal inference is defending the assumption that the measured covariates close every backdoor path — choosing the adjustment set, not the arithmetic that follows.
Potential Outcomes Structural Causal Models
Primitive object Each unit i has fixed counterfactuals Yi(0), Yi(1) DAG plus structural equations Y := fY(T, X, UY), T := fT(X, UT)
Observation problem Only Yi(Ti) is observed; the other column is missing Joint distribution P(X, T, Y); no privileged column
Intervention Set Ti = t for every unit and ask for Yi(t) do(T = t): delete T's equation, replace with T = t — graph surgery
Identifying assumption Conditional ignorability: {Y(0), Y(1)} ⊥ T | X X satisfies the backdoor criterion for (T, Y)
Estimand ATE = E[Y(1) − Y(0)] E[Y | do(T = 1)] − E[Y | do(T = 0)]
Estimator Stratify on X; difference of means within strata, weighted by P(X) Backdoor adjustment Σx P(Y | T, X = x)·P(X = x)
Take-home. Potential outcomes and SCMs are translations, not rivals. Conditional ignorability and the backdoor criterion are the same assumption stated in different vocabularies. The stratification estimator and the backdoor adjustment formula are the same arithmetic. POF makes the missing-data structure of causal inference vivid; SCMs make the algebra of confounding and intervention transparent. Pick whichever language makes your problem easiest to think about.
